01

Research question

Does the enjoyment people get from watching football fall as they watch more matches, and is the pattern different for highly engaged fans and casual viewers?

The economics in one minute

Utility is satisfaction. Marginal utility is the extra satisfaction from one more unit: here, one more match. The law of diminishing marginal utility says each extra unit usually adds less than the one before. Football is an interesting test because a passionate fan might not follow the rule: anticipation, loyalty, rivalries and the importance of a particular fixture can keep enjoyment high.

H1. Within a person, reported enjoyment declines with the order in which matches are watched in a 7-day window.
H2. The decline is smaller for highly engaged fans.
Robustness. H1 should survive controlling for match importance and whether the viewer's favourite team played.

Questionnaire: three engagement items (1-5 agreement), the number of matches watched in the previous 7 days, and for each match in order watched (up to five): enjoyment (1-10), importance (1-5) and whether the respondent's favourite team played. Variables: order = position of the match in the respondent's week; enjoyment = self-rated 1-10 (proxy for marginal utility); engagement = mean of three items, "highly engaged" ≥ 4.0 (set before analysis).

Cleaning: exact duplicates removed; respondents with no matches have no ratings; ratings outside 1-10 dropped. Full log in exclusion_log.txt.

Limitations

  • Self-reported ratings from memory (recall bias).
  • Convenience sample, not representative of all football fans.
  • Small sample, so wide uncertainty at later match positions.
  • Survey data show association, not causation: a fall in enjoyment could reflect match quality, fatigue or the order in which people chose to watch.

Reproducible analysis

All statistics and charts on this page are produced by one script. To reproduce: pip install pandas numpy scipy matplotlib then python analysis/analyze.py data/responses.csv.

  • Mean enjoyment by match order with t-based 95% CIs.
  • Per-person OLS slope of enjoyment on match order; one-sample t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test against 0.
  • H2: Welch t-test, Mann-Whitney test and bootstrap CI (5,000 resamples) for the difference in mean slopes between groups. With so few people, H1 is also checked with a sign-flip permutation test.
  • Robustness: person fixed-effects regression with cluster-robust standard errors, controlling for importance and favourite-team flag.

Conclusions and implications

Draft, edit in your own words. This study does not find reliable evidence of diminishing marginal utility in football viewing. Average enjoyment dips from the first to the second match (7.4 to 6.2) but then recovers, so there is no monotonic decline. The average within-person slope is negative (about -0.67 points per extra match), but with ten people its confidence interval comfortably includes zero.

H2 is directionally consistent (highly engaged fans declined less) but the intervals overlap massively, so it is inconclusive, not confirmed.

The clearest result is not about order at all: matches involving the respondent's favourite team were rated about 1.2 points higher within the same person, and this survived the cluster-robust interval. Who is playing appears to matter more than how many matches came before. That is itself an economic point: utility from a match depends on the match's attributes, not just cumulative consumption.

What this cannot show: causation, representativeness, or true utility (enjoyment is a self-report). Three respondents gave a stated match count that disagrees with the number of matches they rated, which signals measurement noise. Next steps: a larger sample (50+ people with 2+ matches), a diary design that records enjoyment right after each match, and matches of comparable importance.
